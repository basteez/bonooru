Bonooru — main screen: 3 layout options

Low-fi wireframes to choose the information architecture of the main screen (RiiDraw-like single screen; the setup & flash wizard stays a separate guided path). Each option is shown in 3 states: empty, song loaded, playing. Song: “Ode to Joy.mid” (public domain).

Grey wireframe only — no colours, brand or type chosen. Frames: 1280×800 window shown at 50 %.Dashed note = octave-folded note. Note labels = fingering (MECH hypothesis: L R A Y X, ▲▼ D-pad, S stick).“?” buttons = Home / Capture / + / − not decided. Setup & flash wizard is a separate path, reached from “Setup & flash…”.

A · Everything on one canvas

Status strip on top. Left: song (open/drop, track, transpose, fit report, tempo). Centre: piano roll + preview transport. Right: gamepad always docked. Bottom bar: big PLAY / STOP and progress.

Pros

  • Nothing is hidden: every function of FR-15 except setup is visible at once; very RiiDraw-like (gamepad is just there).
  • PLAY/STOP always in the same place, easy to hit fast — the single most important action has a fixed home.
  • Clear left-to-right reading order: song → roll → gamepad → play.

Cons

  • Busiest screen; at 1280 px the piano roll only gets ~650 px, and less on small laptops.
  • Gamepad takes a whole column even when it is useless (song editing, playing).
  • No explicit hint about order (gamepad first, then Play) beyond a one-line helper text.

A.5 first-time user

  • Helps: discoverability — a novice sees the gamepad without looking for it.
  • Hurts: “what do I do first?” — three equal-weight panels, no guided order.
  • Helps: safety note sits next to STOP, where eyes are during play.

B · Piano roll first, gamepad as a drawer

The piano roll fills the window. Song controls are a compact top toolbar. The gamepad is a right-side drawer toggled by a button or the G key. PLAY floats bottom-right. Status lives in a thin bottom status bar.

Pros

  • Biggest piano roll — best for reading fingerings, folded notes and the playhead.
  • Calm, modern feel; closest to a media player.
  • Gamepad can be opened with G from anywhere, so power users keep their hands on the keyboard.

Cons

  • Hidden things get missed: a closed drawer and a toolbar chip are easy to overlook.
  • Toolbar gets crowded (track, transpose, report, tempo, preview) and may wrap on narrow windows.
  • Status bar at the bottom is small; a lost link or version mismatch could go unnoticed.
  • Floating button can cover the end of the song in the roll.

A.5 first-time user

  • Hurts: finding the gamepad if the drawer is closed — needs the open-by-default rule shown in state 1.
  • Hurts: fit report is a chip, so “what doesn’t fit” (FR-04) is one click away instead of visible.
  • Helps: a single dominant canvas means less to scan once the song is loaded.

C · Single screen with a step rail

One screen, but a left rail with 3 numbered steps: 1 Connect & reach free play (gamepad), 2 Pick a song, 3 Play. The active step expands, the others collapse to a summary line. The piano roll on the right is always there (drop zone when empty).

Pros

  • Built-in order: tells a novice exactly what to do next without a separate wizard.
  • Still one screen — steps are not locked, the user can jump (drop a file, reopen gamepad).
  • Collapsed summaries double as a status recap (in free play · song · transpose · tempo).

Cons

  • The app cannot detect free play (U9), so step 1 relies on a manual “I’m in free play” click.
  • Feels like a wizard to repeat users; the confirm click becomes noise on the 10th song.
  • Gamepad is hidden once step 1 collapses — re-entering free play after a game exit needs a reopen.
  • STOP sits in the rail, not in a fixed screen spot like A.

A.5 first-time user

  • Helps most with “what do I do first?” — numbered steps mirror the README.
  • Helps: gamepad is the first thing shown, so reaching free play is not skipped.
  • Risk: user may click “I’m in free play” without being there; Play then fails or plays into a menu.
